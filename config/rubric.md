# Starjump: editor's rubric

Edit this file to change what Claude picks. Plain English works best.

## What Starjump is

A daily email about progress: real advances that make life better, cheaper, longer, safer or freer. Readers are young Britons. The editor favours individual liberty, the rule of law, property rights, low tax, free trade and building things.

## Where stories come from

- Britain comes first. When a British and a foreign story are equally strong, pick the British one. Aim for about half the shortlist to be British.
- Global advances are welcome, especially in science, medicine, technology, energy and climate. A big breakthrough anywhere beats a small British one.

## A story passes if

- Something got better, cheaper, faster, freer, cleaner or safer, or a person, team or firm achieved something real.
- It has already happened: a result, a launch, a trial outcome, a record, a deal signed, construction started. Promises, targets and plans do not count.
- The good part is concrete: a number, a place, a name.

## Prefer

- Scientific and medical breakthroughs: new treatments, trial results, discoveries.
- Technology that works: launches, records, firsts, things getting cheaper.
- Energy and climate progress: cheaper clean power, nuclear, batteries, emissions falling while output grows.
- Homes, power stations, reactors, rail, roads or labs approved or built.
- Tax cuts or simpler tax. Rules, bans or red tape scrapped.
- Private investment, new jobs, firms growing or listing in London.
- Export records and trade deals.
- Crime, poverty or disease falling, with numbers.
- British wins in sport, business, culture or invention.
- At most one human-interest story a day, such as a rescue or an act of kindness.

## Reject

- Government press releases and ministerial announcements, including GOV.UK. Allow one only if it reports a concrete result covered nowhere else, such as a tax cut taking effect.
- New benefits, handouts, grants or subsidies presented as wins.
- Spending pledges, strategies, reviews, consultations and promises.
- New taxes, levies, regulators, quangos or bans.
- Discount or perk schemes for particular groups.
- Opinion pieces, partisan point-scoring, celebrity gossip, deaths.
- Hype: "could", "may one day", early lab results with no clear path to use.
- Stories where the good news is only that something got less bad, unless it is a clear trend backed by numbers.

## Mix

- Prefer free-to-read sources over paywalled ones.
- No more than three stories from the same source.
- Never two stories about the same event.
- Spread the ten across fields: not all science, not all business.

## Headlines

- Rewrite each headline in plain words, 12 words or fewer.
- Lead with the good part.
- For foreign stories, say where, for example "Japanese team..." or "In Kenya...".
- British spelling. No clickbait, no exclamation marks, no question marks, no dashes.
